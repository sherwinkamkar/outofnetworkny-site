Frequently asked questions Out-of-network arbitration questions, answered.

Short answers on eligibility, fees, deadlines, Medicare and Medicaid, and collecting awards. Current through November 5, 2026.

The questions providers ask most

What is the difference between New York IDR and federal IDR?

They cover different plans. New York IDR, under Financial Services Law Article 6, covers fully insured New York plans and, since August 26, 2026, the Empire Plan and Student Employee Health Plan. Federal IDR, under the No Surprises Act, covers self-funded employer plans and insured plans not governed by a state process. For a fully insured New York plan, New York’s process generally applies instead of the federal one. The processes also weigh different factors and have different fees and deadlines.

How do I tell whether a plan is fully insured or self-funded?

The card or EOB may say “self-funded,” “self-insured,” or “administered by” a carrier, which suggests a self-funded plan. Large employers usually self-fund. The plan line matters more than the carrier, because the same carrier often runs both kinds. When in doubt, the plan can confirm it, and we check it as part of every review.

What is a surprise bill under New York law?

Generally, a bill from an out-of-network physician for services at an in-network hospital or ambulatory surgery center where an in-network physician was unavailable, or the out-of-network physician provided services without the patient’s knowledge, or unforeseen circumstances arose. It also covers certain referrals by an in-network physician made without the patient’s signed acknowledgment. It does not cover a patient who chose an out-of-network physician when an in-network one was available.

Can an anesthesiologist or radiologist have the patient consent to a balance bill?

Not at an in-network facility. Federal rules bar anesthesia, radiology, pathology and lab, emergency medicine, neonatology, assistant surgeons, hospitalists, and intensivists from using the notice-and-consent waiver (45 C.F.R. § 149.420). The payment dispute runs against the plan.

What does a New York reviewer consider?

The factors in FSL § 604, including the 80th percentile of charges for the service in the area from FAIR Health data, any gross disparity between the fee and what the plan pays similar providers, the physician’s training and experience, the physician’s usual charge, case complexity, and patient characteristics. State Plan disputes use a separate 50th-percentile allowed-amount benchmark.

What does a federal reviewer consider?

The qualifying payment amount and additional circumstances such as the provider’s training and experience, patient acuity, market share, and prior contracted rates. The QPA gets no presumption. Billed charges, usual and customary charges, and public-payer rates such as Medicare cannot be considered.

What are the filing fees?

In New York there is no government fee; the reviewer’s fee is $300 to $495 per dispute depending on the entity, paid up front by both sides and refunded to the winner within 30 days. Federally, the administrative fee is $15 per party per dispute (disputes initiated on or after June 11, 2026), and reviewer fees run $200 to $840 for a single claim and $268 to $1,173 for a batch of up to 25 line items. The losing side ends up paying the reviewer in both systems.

Is there a deadline to file?

Federal deadlines are short and strict: open negotiation must start within 30 business days after the initial payment or denial, and IDR within 4 business days after negotiation ends. New York’s Article 6 does not set a fixed filing deadline, but older claims are harder to document, and separate contract claims are generally subject to New York’s six-year limit, which plan terms can shorten. We check the dates on every claim before advising.

How long does the process take?

In New York, the decision is generally due within 45 business days after the reviewer has all the information it needs. Federally, the statutory steps take roughly five to six months from the initial payment to a decision and payment, and backlogs and eligibility disputes can add time.

Can I batch claims?

Federally, yes, within the batching rules. For disputes whose open negotiation begins on or after November 1, 2026, a batch can include up to 50 line items. In New York, each dispute is filed through the DFS portal; we organize related claims to keep costs down.

Do Medicare or Medicaid claims qualify?

No. Neither New York IDR nor the No Surprises Act covers Medicare, Medicare Advantage, or Medicaid. A provider not contracted with a Medicare Advantage plan must accept the Original Medicare amount as payment in full, and can dispute an underpayment with the plan and then CMS’s Payment Dispute Resolution Contractor. Medicaid managed care disputes filed on or after May 28, 2026 are excluded from New York IDR.

What if the plan does not pay the award?

New York determinations are binding and admissible in court, and we pursue unpaid ones with DFS and in state court. For federal awards, the Second Circuit held in September 2026 that the No Surprises Act does not let providers sue to enforce an award, so unpaid awards go to CMS, the Department of Labor, or the state regulator.

Does the firm guarantee a result?

No. Every dispute turns on its record, and prior results do not guarantee a similar outcome. What we can promise is a candid assessment of which claims are worth pursuing.

How are your fees structured?

We handle surprise billing arbitration on a contingency basis under a written engagement letter. The letter sets the percentage and explains how filing costs are handled. The initial claims review is free.
